Week: 13
Task: Review a ninety-day build, and say what actually changed about prompting
Agent: Claude (Claude Code, terminal)

Prompt or workflow:
Closing log. The challenge asked for 30 components and 13 agent logs over
ninety days; the library finished at 70 components, each carrying the prompt
that produced it, two recorded failed attempts, and a note on why the failure
was interesting. This log is the summary of what the prompts taught.

Result — the five rules that survived, in the order they were learned:

1. Never name the fix. A prompt that says "build the rows with innerHTML" has
   a diagnosis in it that nobody checked. Two deploys and forty minutes went
   into a fix aimed at JavaScript that was 1.2% of the problem. "Measure where
   the time goes" has no theory in it and cannot be wrong that way.

2. State the claim as a number. "Test it" produces a test of the code. "Four
   locales must produce four different orders" produces a test of the claim —
   and returned 2 of 4 on a component that had been passing for hours.

3. Name the adverse condition, not the feature. "Make the toolbar responsive"
   produces breakpoints. "Make sure it works at 400% zoom" produces a
   ResizeObserver, because the second rules out the first answer.

4. Ask what would have caught it. When the same class of mistake happens twice,
   the output of the conversation should be a rule or a test, not a patch. The
   six-word question "why are you not finding them yourself" produced a skill
   and a four-rule QA suite that then caught a clipped headline, an invisible
   click-blocking element, and a token collision.

5. Measure the thing you are choosing, not its specification. The font with
   the widest nominal width axis condensed half as much as one with a narrower
   range. Four candidates, one metric, four minutes.

The pattern under all five: the prompts that worked removed an assumption
rather than adding an instruction. The ones that failed were confident,
specific, and contained an unverified belief about the cause.

What it saved: the challenge's own framing was that it exists to teach the team
to prompt LLMs well. The honest answer after ninety days is that prompt length
and politeness changed nothing measurable, and that the single highest-value
habit was refusing to state a cause I had not measured. Everything else follows
from that.

Carrying forward: the build-time gate (missing prompt fails the build), the
visual QA suite, the flush-to-disk progress rule, and the account verification
step before any deploy. All four are in the project skill rather than in
anyone's memory, which is the only reason they will still be there in March.
